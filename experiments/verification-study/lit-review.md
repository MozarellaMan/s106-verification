# Literature review: can agreement among AI readers, evidence checks and follow-up decide when a research answer is correct?

Scope: this section reviews the work behind each part of the audited system. The system uses three readers from different model families, an accuracy estimate from pairwise agreement, a bootstrap lower bound, a 90% gate, and a "confirmed" label when posterior error is at most 1%. Reference status is in the list at the end. "Verified" means the paper's existence, authors and venue appeared in search results. I read the full text of one paper (Platanios et al. 2014); everything else rests on abstracts and catalogue pages.

## 1. Estimating accuracy without ground truth

**Established.** Dawid and Skene (1979) introduced the first latent-class model of annotator error. Each annotator has a confusion matrix, the true label is hidden, and EM fits both. Raykar et al. (2010) added a classifier trained jointly with the annotators. Zhang, Chen, Zhou and Jordan (2014/2016) proved that a spectral initialisation followed by EM reaches near-minimax error under the Dawid-Skene model. These papers assume that annotators err independently given the true label.

A separate line asks for accuracy directly, with no labels. Platanios, Blum and Mitchell (2014) relate the agreement rate of two classifiers to their error rates. In the binary case, a_ij = 1 - e_i - e_j + 2 e_ij, where e_ij is the joint error rate (equation 5 in the paper; I read it). If errors are independent, e_ij = e_i e_j. Three classifiers then give three equations in three unknowns, and the paper solves them in closed form. It requires better-than-chance accuracy to pick the root. Its abstract says the method also works with at least three approximations, and that dependent errors need more classifiers or an extra optimisation criterion. Platanios, Dubey and Mitchell (2016) made this Bayesian, with priors over accuracies and error dependence. Platanios, Poon, Mitchell and Horvitz (2017) added logical constraints between target classes, which supply extra equations. Parisi et al. (2014) and Jaffe, Nadler and Kluger (2015) use spectral methods. With conditionally independent classifiers, the off-diagonal covariance matrix is rank one, and its leading eigenvector gives balanced accuracies. Jaffe et al. add a third-order tensor view that matches the triplet idea.

**Identifiability.** All of these need at least three raters that are conditionally independent given the true label, and each better than chance. With two raters the data give one equation for two unknowns. With three the model is just identified, so nothing in the data tests the independence assumption. A fourth rater adds checks. Platanios (2014) shows that with more raters you can relax independence at the cost of more equations.

**How the audited estimator differs.** The estimator a_i = sqrt(A_ij A_ik / A_jk) assumes two raters agree only when both are right. Then A_ij = a_i a_j, and the square-root formula follows. That drops the "both wrong and agree" term. In the binary case the full relation is A_ij = a_i a_j + (1 - a_i)(1 - a_j) when errors are independent, which is the same as the equation above with e_ij = e_i e_j. For many-valued answers, wrong answers can still coincide, and how often depends on how concentrated the wrong answers are. The simplified form therefore overstates accuracy whenever raters share wrong answers, and the size of the bias depends on the answer space. I found no paper that treats this exact simplification; this is my reading of the algebra, not a citation. The related literature handles it either with full confusion matrices (Dawid-Skene) or by modelling joint errors (Platanios).

**Difficulty and correlation.** Whitehill et al. (2009, GLAD) put item difficulty into the model, with one latent difficulty per item and one ability per labeler. Hard items drive all raters wrong together, which breaks conditional independence in a way the plain Dawid-Skene model cannot absorb. Under difficulty variation, pairwise agreement is higher than independence predicts, and the triplet estimate overstates accuracy. Platanios (2014) and Jaffe et al. (2015, as I read the abstract; a separate Jaffe et al. paper on classifier dependencies appeared in 2015, which I did not verify) both note this as the main failure mode.

**Summary.** Established: with three conditionally independent, better-than-chance raters, accuracy is identifiable from agreement alone, and there are consistent and Bayesian estimators. Comparable to our setting: the triplet formula is a special case of Platanios (2014) and of the spectral approach. Gap: the literature gives no guarantee when raters share a base model, a corpus or a failure mode, which is the usual case for LLMs, and I found no work on a bootstrap lower bound used as a release gate with a fixed accuracy threshold.

## 2. Correlated errors across LLMs

**Established.** Kim, Garg, Peng and Garg (ICML 2025) tested over 350 LLMs on two leaderboards and a resume-screening task. On one leaderboard, two models agree 60% of the time when both are wrong. Correlation rises with shared architecture and provider, and larger, more accurate models are more correlated even across providers. Goel et al. (ICML 2025) built the CAPA metric, which scores similarity by overlapping mistakes. They found that LLM judges favour models similar to themselves, that weak-to-strong gains depend on complementary knowledge, and that errors of frontier models are becoming more alike as capability grows. Panickssery, Bowman and Feng (NeurIPS 2024) found that LLM evaluators recognise their own outputs and score them higher, and that fine-tuning for self-recognition strengthens the bias. Zheng et al. (2023) reported self-enhancement, position and verbosity biases in LLM judges.

Self-consistency (Wang et al., ICLR 2023) shows that sampling several reasoning paths and taking the majority beats greedy decoding. It helps only when errors across samples are not perfectly correlated, and samples from one model share that model's blind spots. Selecting readers from different families lowers the correlation but does not remove it.

**Summary.** Established: errors across current LLMs are positively correlated, and the correlation grows with capability. Comparable: "three readers from different families" is the standard mitigation, and these papers measure how far it falls short. Gap: they report correlation on public benchmarks, not on long documents with extracted evidence, and none ties the measured correlation to bias in an agreement-based accuracy estimate. That link is the one an audit would need.

## 3. Scalable oversight

**Established.** Irving, Christiano and Amodei (2018) proposed debate: two agents argue and a weaker judge decides. Khan et al. (ICML 2024) tested it: non-expert LLM judges reached 76% accuracy with debate against 48% for a naive baseline, and human judges 88% against 60% (on QuALITY, per the abstract). Bowman et al. (2022) proposed measuring oversight by having humans with an unreliable model assistant answer hard questions; the humans did better than either the model or themselves alone. Burns et al. (ICML 2024) showed that a strong model fine-tuned on a weak model's labels beats the weak supervisor but falls short of its own ceiling. I did not search for market making, recursive reward modelling or Anthropic's automated auditing work, and I do not cite them.

**Summary.** Established: a weaker judge can sometimes recover truth from stronger parties, given an adversarial or informative structure. Comparable: our setting is also a weak-checks-strong problem, since several readers judge claims they could not produce themselves. Gap: these papers measure judge accuracy against known answers. They do not estimate accuracy when no answer key exists, and they say little about when to stop and accept a result.

## 4. Calibration, selective prediction and abstention

**Established.** Kadavath et al. (2022) found that larger models are fairly well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions in the right format, and can predict whether they know an answer. Xiong et al. (ICLR 2024) compared ways to elicit confidence in words and found that verbalised confidence tends to be overconfident; consistency across samples helps. Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar (ICLR 2023) and Farquhar et al. (Nature 2024) compute entropy over meaning clusters of sampled answers, which separates confabulation from variation in wording. Geifman and El-Yaniv (NIPS 2017) define selective classification with a target risk level, and El-Yaniv and Wiener (JMLR 2010) give the risk-coverage framing. Quach et al. (ICLR 2024) extend conformal prediction to language generation, returning a set of samples with a coverage guarantee and a stopping rule.

**Summary.** Established: confidence signals exist and can drive abstention; conformal and selective methods give a formal route to "answer only when risk is below x". Comparable: the 90% gate and the 1% posterior cell rule are risk-controlled abstention. Gap: those guarantees rest on exchangeable calibration data with labels. An unlabeled agreement estimate replaces the labels, and nobody has shown that the resulting bound keeps its coverage when its independence assumption fails.

## 5. Factuality and evidence-grounded evaluation

**Established.** FEVER (Thorne et al., NAACL 2018) set up claim verification against retrieved evidence; its baseline pipeline got 31.87% on label plus evidence. FActScore (Min et al., EMNLP 2023) splits text into atomic facts and scores the supported share; ChatGPT scored 58% on biographies. SAFE and LongFact (Wei et al., NeurIPS 2024) use an LLM agent with search; SAFE agreed with crowd annotators 72% of the time and won 76% of sampled disagreements. Rashkin et al. (Computational Linguistics 2023) defined Attributable to Identified Sources (AIS). Gao et al. (EMNLP 2023, ALCE) found that even the best models lacked full citation support about half the time on ELI5. Liu, Zhang and Liang (Findings EMNLP 2023) found that only 51.5% of sentences from four generative search engines were fully supported by citations, and 74.5% of citations supported their sentence. Walters and Wilder (Scientific Reports 2023) found that 55% of GPT-3.5 and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated, with further errors in real ones. Magesh et al. (2024, J. Empirical Legal Studies 2025) found that commercial legal research tools hallucinated in at least 17% of queries. For support checking, TRUE (Honovich et al., NAACL 2022) found that NLI-based and question-generation-based checkers do well and are complementary, and AlignScore (Zha et al., ACL 2023) trains one alignment model on 4.7M examples. ContractNLI (Koreeda and Manning, Findings EMNLP 2021) asks for entailment plus evidence spans over 607 contracts, and found that exceptions that negate obligations make it hard. CUAD (Hendrycks et al., NeurIPS 2021) offers over 13,000 expert annotations, and LegalBench (Guha et al., NeurIPS 2023) has 162 tasks.

**Summary.** Established: AI answers often cite sources that do not support them; checking each claim against its cited span is a known method, and the error rates of the checkers are themselves substantial. Comparable: our evidence check is the same operation as AIS, ALCE and SAFE, applied to contracts as in ContractNLI. Gap: these benchmarks score checkers against human labels. They do not say how often a checker and the answer reader fail together, which is what decides whether "evidence found" adds information beyond agreement.

## 6. Multi-agent verification, self-correction and adaptive compute

**Established.** Chain-of-Verification (Dhuliawala et al., 2023) drafts an answer, plans check questions, answers them independently and revises; it cut hallucination on list questions and long-form text. Du et al. (ICML 2024) let several model instances critique each other over rounds and report gains on factuality and arithmetic. Huang et al. (ICLR 2024) found that models cannot reliably self-correct reasoning without external feedback, and that some earlier gains came from oracle labels or weak first prompts. Their critique also says debate does not beat self-consistency at equal numbers of responses. FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, 2023; TMLR 2024) cascades from cheap to costly models and reports matching GPT-4 with up to 98% lower cost. Madras, Pitassi and Zemel (NeurIPS 2018) formalise learning to defer, where a model passes hard cases to another decision-maker.

**Summary.** Established: independent re-checking helps; self-checking without outside signal often does not; cascades save cost when a cheap signal predicts when to escalate. Comparable: targeted follow-up on disputed cells is a cascade whose escalation signal is disagreement. Gap: published cascades use confidence or a trained router and are scored on labeled sets. I found no study that triggers escalation by estimated reader disagreement and checks whether the escalated answers were in fact the wrong ones.

## 7. Document AI and OCR error

**Established.** The OHRBench study (Zhang et al., arXiv 2412.02592) found that OCR errors cut retrieval-augmented generation quality, by at least 14% (5 F1 points) even with the best OCR tool tested, and that no current OCR solution suffices for a high-quality knowledge base. It separates semantic noise from formatting noise.

**Summary.** Established: parsing errors propagate to downstream answers. Comparable: all readers in our system read the same OCR text, so an OCR error is a shared input error that agreement cannot see. Gap: I did not survey this area beyond one paper. The relevant point is structural: errors upstream of all readers are perfectly correlated across them.

## Where a genuine gap plausibly remains

Citations, retrieval, OCR, majority agreement and abstention gates are not new, and this report should not claim them. The following looks open, with the caveat that my search was shallow and covered only titles and abstracts.

1. **Validity of the agreement estimator under shared failure.** The estimator theory assumes conditionally independent raters. The LLM literature shows they are not independent. I found no empirical audit that compares an agreement-based accuracy estimate and its lower bound against a labeled set, per answer type, for LLM readers. The size and sign of the bias are unknown.
2. **The "agree only when both right" simplification.** Its bias depends on how concentrated wrong answers are. A study that varies answer-space size and measures that bias would be new, if it does not exist in a source I missed.
3. **Using the estimate as a release gate.** Selective classification and conformal methods get their guarantees from labeled calibration data. An unlabeled estimate with a bootstrap bound has no such guarantee, and a "confirmed at 1% posterior error" rule inherits whatever mis-calibration the estimate has. Testing the gate's realised error on held-out labeled cells is the direct audit.
4. **Evidence check plus agreement.** Whether the two signals are independent enough to multiply is untested. If the evidence checker is built from the same model families as the readers, they likely fail together.
5. **Follow-up targeted by disagreement.** It is plausible but unevaluated whether cells chosen by disagreement have higher error than random cells, and by how much.
